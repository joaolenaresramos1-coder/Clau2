**A fórmula (comum aos três):**
1. **Abertura em 2ª pessoa** ("Picture this…"): coloca o espectador dentro da cena, com frases curtas, silêncio e tensão.
2. **Reviravolta logo no começo** ("Here's the strange part…"): o perigo não é o que você pensa.
3. **Promessa** ("By the end of this, you'll know…"): uma lista de 3 a 4 perguntas que o vídeo vai responder.
4. **Frase-título falada**: "So let's talk about why you'd never want to…".
5. **Anatomia e comportamento**, com números exatos e **comparações do dia a dia** ("deeper than 3 Eiffel Towers", "younger than your phone").
6. **Ganchos para depois** ("Remember the claws." / "Remember that."), que pagam mais tarde.
7. **Casos reais com nome, data e lugar**, além de checagem de mitos ("We couldn't find a scientific source for that number").
8. **Segmento interativo**: "So what would you actually do?" → cenário detalhado → 3 opções → "Pick one before I tell you." → a resposta instintiva é a pior → passo a passo correto → reviravolta.
9. **Virada de empatia e ecologia**: clima, quem está de fato em perigo.
10. **Fechamento**: repete a frase-título, retoma a cena da abertura e termina com uma frase de efeito.
11. **CTA fixo**: "Anyway, thanks for watching this one… let me know in the comments… check it out here."

**Visual:** animação 2D flat, paleta azul-petróleo escura, mergulhador estilizado, textos curtos na tela (números grandes, rótulos), mapas bege, linhas do tempo e algumas fotos reais com crédito. O canal se chama "Depth Theory" e termina com a tela "WATCH NEXT".

## 2. Tema escolhido

**Água-viva-caixa (Chironex fleckeri) e Irukandji.** Encaixa perfeitamente no formato 1 e 2:
- Abertura em 2ª pessoa: uma praia perfeita e vazia no norte da Austrália.
- Reviravolta: o animal mais venenoso do mundo não tem cérebro, mas tem 24 olhos. E o mais perigoso é o que você nem sente (Irukandji, do tamanho de uma unha).
- Casos reais: Hugo Flecker em 1955 e Jack Barnes, que em 1961 picou a si mesmo, o filho e um salva-vidas.
- Segmento interativo: água doce, arrancar os tentáculos ou vinagre? E a reviravolta: o primeiro passo é a RCP.
- Ganchos: o poste com a garrafa (vinagre) e as meias-calças dos salva-vidas.

## 3. NARRAÇÃO (texto limpo para o gerador de voz)

> Em inglês, como nos vídeos de referência. ≈2.800 palavras, ≈16 minutos em ritmo de narração.

Picture this. It's late afternoon in December, on a beach in far north Queensland, Australia.
The sand is white. The water is flat. It's over thirty degrees in the shade.
The sea is so clear you can see your own feet.

You wade in up to your waist. It's warm. Like a bath.

And there's nobody else in the water. Not one person. On a beach this perfect.

By the path, there's a post stuck in the sand with a plastic bottle hanging off it. You don't read the sign.

You push off and start to swim.

Something brushes across the back of your legs. Soft. Like a wet piece of string.

Then it feels like someone just pressed a hot iron against your skin.

You look down, and long red lines are rising across your legs, like you've been whipped.

You're ten meters from the shore. You start swimming back.

And somewhere around five meters, your legs stop doing what you tell them to.

Here's the strange part. The animal that just did this has no brain. No bones. No heart. No blood. It's about ninety-five percent water.
And in Australia alone, it's been blamed for around seventy deaths since records began.

This is Chironex fleckeri. The Australian box jellyfish. A lot of scientists call it the most venomous animal on Earth.

But it's not the one that worries the people who study it most. That one is about the size of your fingernail. And when it stings you, you'll barely feel it.

By the end of this, you'll know what that bottle on the post was for. Why lifeguards up here used to go to work in pantyhose. How an animal with no brain can see you coming. And exactly what you'd do if the person next to you got stung.

So let's talk about why you'd never want to meet a box jellyfish.

First, what is it?

Look at a normal jellyfish and the bell is round, like an umbrella. Look at this one from the side, and it's a cube. That's where the name comes from.
The biggest ones are about thirty centimeters across. A little bigger than a basketball.

From each of its four corners hangs a bunch of up to fifteen tentacles. That's up to sixty tentacles in total, and each one can stretch out to about three meters. Longer than a car.

And it's almost completely see-through. Pale blue, in water that's pale blue. Even people who look for them for a living say they're incredibly hard to spot.

Those tentacles are lined with stinging cells. Millions of them.
Each one is a tiny capsule with a coiled-up harpoon inside, held under enormous pressure. Touch the trigger, and the capsule fires.
In a tiny freshwater relative, scientists timed that firing as one of the fastest movements in all of biology. Over in less than a millionth of a second.
The harpoon punches through skin and pumps venom in.

So why does a jellyfish need venom this strong?

It hunts prawns and small fish. And its body is incredibly delicate. A fish thrashing around on a tentacle could rip it to pieces. So the venom has to stop it almost instantly.
We just happen to be big animals that wander into the same water.

What does the venom actually do?

Some of its toxins punch holes in cells. Skin cells die, which is why the stings leave scars that look like whip marks, sometimes for life.
And in a bad sting, the venom goes after the heart. It can stop it within minutes. A severe sting can kill an adult before they even make it back to the beach.

How much does it take? Estimates vary. But contact with just a few meters of tentacle may be enough to kill a grown adult. For a child, a lot less. And a lot of the people it has killed were children.

Now here's the part that sounds made up.

A box jellyfish can see.

Hanging from each side of the bell is a tiny club called a rhopalium. Each club holds six eyes. Four of them are simple light sensors. But two of them have a lens, a retina, and a cornea. Like a camera.
That's twenty-four eyes in total. And no brain to send the pictures to.

They use them. Unlike most jellyfish, a box jellyfish doesn't just drift. It swims, on purpose. It steers around posts and rocks, and it heads for the light along the shoreline, where its food gathers.

In 2023, scientists showed that a tiny Caribbean box jellyfish can even learn. Put in a tank with faint gray walls, it kept bumping into them. After a few minutes, it learned to turn away before it hit.
No brain. Just around a thousand nerve cells in each of those little clubs.

So where do they all come from? And why was that beach empty?

For most of the year, you won't find a single adult box jellyfish. They start their lives as tiny polyps, stuck to rocks up in mangrove creeks and river mouths.
When the wet season starts, each polyp turns into a tiny jellyfish and swims out to sea. Then they grow fast. In a few months, they go from a few millimeters to the size of a basketball. They breed. And by winter, they're gone.

That's why northern Australia has a stinger season, roughly from October or November to May.
And that's why a perfect beach in December can be completely empty.

For a long time, nobody knew what was killing people up here.

In the early nineteen hundreds, swimmers in tropical Australia would sometimes just collapse in shallow water and die, sometimes within minutes. Doctors blamed heart attacks. Or drowning. Or the Portuguese man o' war.

Then, in January nineteen fifty-five, a five-year-old boy died after being stung in shallow water at Cardwell, in north Queensland.
A doctor in Cairns named Hugo Flecker had spent years trying to solve this mystery. After the boy's death, the water where he was stung was searched with nets. And they pulled out a big, box-shaped jellyfish that no scientist had ever described.
It was named after him. Chironex fleckeri. Chironex roughly means "hand of death."

But Flecker had another mystery he never solved.

Back in nineteen fifty-two, he had written about a strange illness in swimmers off Cairns. They'd feel a sting. A small one. Almost nothing.
Then, about half an hour later, it would start. Crushing pain in the back. Cramps. Vomiting. Sweating. And a terrible feeling that they were about to die.
He named it after the Aboriginal people of that coast. Irukandji syndrome.
And nobody could find the animal causing it.

That took another doctor, Jack Barnes, in nineteen sixty-one.
He went looking in the water off Cairns, and he caught a tiny box jellyfish, about the size of a fingernail.
And to prove it was the culprit, he stung himself with it. Then his nine-year-old son. Then a local lifeguard.
All three of them ended up in hospital. All three of them survived. And the jellyfish was named after him. Carukia barnesi.

And here's where the danger flips.

The big box jellyfish mostly sticks to the coast. Beaches, river mouths, shallow water. You can build a net around a beach to keep it out. And that's exactly what Queensland did. Stinger nets.

Irukandji is different.
Its body is about a centimeter across. Small enough to slip through a lot of stinger nets. And it doesn't just live at the beach. It turns up out on the reef, and in open water. Right where tourists go snorkeling.

The sting is the trick. Lots of people barely notice it. A little red patch. Maybe nothing at all. They get back on the boat and they feel fine.

Then the clock runs out.
About thirty minutes later, the pain starts. In the back, the chest, the stomach. It comes in waves. Some people need so much pain relief that they're kept in hospital for days. In the worst cases, blood pressure goes so high it can cause bleeding in the brain, or heart failure.
And patients describe the same thing again and again. A feeling of doom. Some of them ask the doctors if they're about to die.

In two thousand and two, two tourists died in Queensland within months of each other. Irukandji was blamed for both.

So how does anyone swim up here at all?

The story goes that back in the nineteen eighties, lifeguards in Queensland would pull on women's pantyhose before going into the water. It sounds like a joke. But it worked, for a simple reason.
Those stinging cells fire when they touch skin. Put even a thin layer of fabric in the way, and most of them can't reach you.
Today, there are full-body lycra stinger suits. There are stinger nets. And on beaches across the north, there are posts. With a bottle of vinegar hanging off them.

Remember that bottle.

So let's put you back on the beach and make it real. What would you actually do?

It's January. You're on a beach outside Cairns with a friend. There's a stinger net, but your friend doesn't like the crowd inside it, so they wade in a little way down the beach. Outside the net.

You're on the sand, looking at your phone. Then you hear the scream.

Your friend stumbles out of the water and falls onto the sand. There are clear, sticky strings still stuck across both legs, like threads of glass. Red welts are already rising underneath them.
They're gasping that it's burning. Then they go quiet.

You've got a cooler with bottled water. There's a vinegar post about thirty meters up the beach. And there's your phone.

So, first move. Do you wash the stings with fresh water and scrub the tentacles off? Pull the tentacles off with your hands? Or run for the vinegar?

Pick one before I tell you.

If you picked fresh water, that's the instinctive choice. And it's the worst one on the list.
Plenty of the stinging cells on those tentacles haven't fired yet. Fresh water changes the balance around them, and many of them go off. Scrubbing does the same. With a towel, with sand, with your hand. You'd be helping the jellyfish inject more venom.

If you picked pulling the tentacles off, you're closer. But grab them before they've been dealt with, and you can make more cells fire, into your friend and into you.

If you picked the vinegar, you're right. But it's not the first thing you do.

Here's how it actually goes.

You shout for help, as loud as you can. You point at someone and tell them to call triple zero. You point at someone else and tell them to get the vinegar.
Then you look at your friend. Are they breathing? Normally?

They're not.

So you start CPR. Right there on the sand. You don't wait for the vinegar. You don't wait for the ambulance.
The venom can stop the heart. CPR keeps the blood moving until help arrives. And people have survived box jellyfish stings because someone did exactly this.

In two thousand and ten, a ten-year-old girl named Rachael Shardlow was stung across both legs in the Calliope River, near Gladstone. She collapsed. Her brother pulled her out of the water. People nearby poured vinegar on the stings.
She survived. And one of Australia's leading jellyfish experts said she may have been the first person known to survive a sting that bad.

Back on your beach, the vinegar arrives. Someone else pours it over the stings, and keeps pouring for at least thirty seconds.
The vinegar doesn't undo the venom that's already in. What it does is stop the stinging cells that haven't fired yet from firing.
Then the tentacles can be lifted off. And if there's no vinegar? Rinse with seawater. Never fresh.

And no, you don't pee on it. It doesn't help, and it can make things worse.

The paramedics arrive. In Australia, there's even a box jellyfish antivenom, made here since nineteen seventy. Doctors still argue about how much it actually helps. But your friend is going to the hospital, and they're going to have scars on their legs for a long time.

And here's the twist.

If it had been Irukandji, none of that would have happened on the beach. No scream. No whip marks. Maybe a small red patch your friend didn't even mention.
They'd have dried off. Had lunch. And half an hour later, in the car, the cramps would have started.

That's why on reef trips up here, crews ask you to tell them about any sting. However small.
The box jellyfish you'd see too late. The Irukandji you'd never see at all.

And they don't only live in Australia.
Box jellyfish live in warm water across the world. Thailand, the Philippines, Indonesia, Japan, Hawaii. In some of those places, deaths aren't always recorded, and some researchers think the real number of people they kill every year is much higher than anyone has counted.
Some scientists also think warmer seas could push Irukandji further south, along more of the Australian coast. That isn't settled yet. But people are watching.

Not everything is scared of them, though.
Sea turtles eat box jellyfish, and seem to shrug off the stings.

And scientists are getting closer to an antidote.
In two thousand and nineteen, researchers in Sydney used gene editing to find out exactly how the venom gets into human cells. It turned out it needs cholesterol to do it.
So they tried a drug that soaks up cholesterol. In mice, given within fifteen minutes of a sting, it blocked the pain and the skin damage.
It isn't a treatment yet. But it's the first real clue in seventy years.

So that's why you'd never want to meet a box jellyfish.

Not because it's a monster. It doesn't have a brain to want anything.
It's a few months old. It's almost all water. It was trying to catch a prawn. And by winter, it'll be dead.

You were just the big thing that swam into its net.

And the one to really worry about isn't even the one with the whip marks. It's the one the size of your fingernail. The one that slips through the net. The one you swim straight through, and don't even feel.

So if you're ever on a perfect beach in the tropics, and the water is warm and clear, and nobody is swimming, read the sign on the post.

Anyway, thanks for watching this one. If there's another animal you'd like us to put you in the water with, let me know in the comments. And if you want to see another video like this one, check it out here.
